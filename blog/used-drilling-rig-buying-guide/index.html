Buying a Used Drilling Rig: A Practical Inspection and Due-Diligence Guide

A used rig can be a great deal or an expensive mistake, and the difference usually comes down to what you checked before you signed. Here's a practical walkthrough of what to inspect and what paperwork to require.

Buying a used land rig, mobile rig, or workover unit is a significant capital decision, and photos plus a spec sheet only tell you so much. A rig that's been properly maintained and stored can run reliably for years past its original service life. One that's been neglected or cannibalized for parts can turn into a money pit fast — and the difference isn't always obvious from a distance.

This isn't a substitute for a qualified mechanical inspection — for any purchase above minor value, get one. But knowing what a good inspection covers, and what questions to ask before you get that far, puts you in a much stronger position at the negotiating table.

Start with the paperwork, not the equipment

Before you spend time or money inspecting a rig, confirm the basics on paper:

  • Clear title and ownership. Confirm the seller actually owns the equipment free and clear, or has authority to sell on behalf of the owner. Liens, disputed ownership, or unclear chain of title can tie up a deal indefinitely.
  • Maintenance and inspection history. A rig with organized service records, API inspection documentation, and a logged maintenance history tells you far more than a rig with none of that — even before you look at a single component.
  • Hours or years in service, and time idle. A rig that's been properly stacked and preserved can sit idle for a long time without major issues. One that's been sitting exposed with no preservation program is a different story, even if the "hours" look similar on paper.

For cross-border or brokered transactions, expect standard commercial documentation — an NCNDA before buyer and seller details are shared, followed by LOIs, purchase agreements, and bills of sale as the deal progresses. This isn't paperwork for its own sake; it protects both sides and keeps the transaction on a track that closes.

The mechanical inspection: what actually matters

A thorough inspection covers the rig system by system. Here's the core of what a qualified inspector — or a knowledgeable buyer doing a serious walk-around — should be checking.

Derrick & substructure

  • Visual and, where possible, non-destructive inspection for structural cracks, corrosion, and weld fatigue, particularly at load-bearing joints
  • Condition of the crown block, traveling block, and hook — wear on sheaves and bearing surfaces
  • Substructure integrity and any signs of prior structural repair or reinforcement (ask for documentation on any repairs)

Drawworks & rotary system

  • Drawworks condition — brake system, clutch or transmission wear, drum and cable condition
  • Rotary table and, on AC/top-drive rigs, top drive condition and service history
  • Evidence of proper lubrication and maintenance versus deferred servicing

Power & electrical system

  • Engine or generator condition, hours, and maintenance records
  • Electrical system condition on SCR/AC rigs — this is often where deferred maintenance shows up first and is expensive to fix
  • Instrumentation and control system functionality

Mud system & pressure control

  • Mud pump condition, including liner and valve wear
  • BOP stack: current certification status, test history, and physical condition — this is a safety-critical component and a major re-certification cost if neglected
  • Tank and solids control equipment condition

Questions to ask before you make an offer

  1. Why is the rig being sold? A straightforward answer (fleet downsizing, operator moving to a different rig class) is different from a vague or evasive one.
  2. Where has it been operating, and where is it now? Storage conditions and climate matter — a rig stacked properly in a dry yard is a different proposition than one left exposed near the coast.
  3. Can I get a third-party mechanical inspection before closing? A seller who resists an independent inspection is telling you something.
  4. What's included, and what isn't? Confirm exactly what components, spares, and documentation transfer with the rig — verbally agreed inclusions that aren't in writing have a way of disappearing.

The bottom line

A used rig purchase comes down to two things: verifying the equipment is actually what it's represented to be, and making sure the paperwork protects you if it isn't. Skip either step to save time, and you're taking on risk that a few extra days of due diligence would have caught. Take both seriously, and a well-priced used rig can be one of the better capital decisions you make this year.

This guide is general information based on standard industry inspection practice and does not substitute for a qualified mechanical or structural inspection specific to the rig you're evaluating. FAQ

Common questions

What's the single most important thing to check on a used drilling rig?

There isn't one silver-bullet item — a proper inspection covers the derrick and substructure, drawworks and rotary system, power/electrical system, mud system, BOP stack, and documentation together. A rig can look sound mechanically and still carry a title or lien problem that kills the deal, so treat structural inspection and paperwork as equally important.

Should I hire a third-party inspector for a used rig purchase?

For any rig above minor value, yes. A qualified third-party mechanical inspector can catch structural fatigue, hidden corrosion, and component wear that isn't visible in photos or a walk-around, and their report gives you a documented basis for price negotiation or walking away.

What documentation should come with a used rig?

At minimum: proof of clear title/ownership, maintenance and inspection records, API certification documents where applicable, and a bill of sale. For cross-border deals, expect an NCNDA and other standard commercial documentation before buyer-seller details are shared.
